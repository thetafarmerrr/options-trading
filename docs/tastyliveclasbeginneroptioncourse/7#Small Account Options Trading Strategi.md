## Key 1: Trade Small, Keep Small Position Sizes
Let's move on to our first key when we're trading in a small account and something that really helped me out when I was trading over these past six to seven months in a smaller account.

Really the main key when we're trading in a small account or a big account — and I think this is the most important thing overall — is to trade small and stay small in our position size. Ideally we would like to allocate between 1 and 2% of total portfolio buying power per trade, or less than 5% if we're taking more aggressive positions.

What does this mean? Well, if I have a $100,000 account, that means that if I'm selling premium or I have a trade on that ties up $5,000 in buying power or less, then I'm staying within this range. But if I only have a $10,000 account, that means I need to focus on trades that tie up around $500 of buying power per trade to stay small enough to withstand adverse price moves. If a bad trade goes against me, I want to make sure that it doesn't totally wipe out or paralyze my account because I traded too large.

When we're looking at smaller accounts, it is going to be harder to trade small within that 1 to 5% range. There are two main tactics that help maintain this tight risk range:

### Tactic 1: Use Defined Risk Strategies
The number one solution is defined risk. We talk about naked options and their benefits, and those benefits are all true, but defined risk trades are an extremely useful tool for smaller accounts because they naturally help us keep position sizes small.

I can trade large underlying stocks like Apple, Amazon, or Google, and I'm able to open three-point wide spreads or five-point wide spreads for around $1.00 or maybe $1.50 if I'm striking closer to the current stock price. This caps my maximum loss per trade to something around $200 or $300, which means that even if the trade moves completely against me, I'm only going to lose that fixed specified amount.

Defined risk structures are certainly key for small accounts; they help us learn the ins and outs of trading while locking in maximum loss so we can sleep well at night. Also, defined risk trades are easy to access from a brokerage perspective. You have to have permission to trade options from the start, and with TDM Trade, defined risk trades only require Tier 2 options approval. We can also use our dojo completion certificate to help speed up Tier 2 approval from TDM Trade. Approval is not guaranteed, but the certificate improves your odds significantly.

### Tactic 2: Trade Low-Priced Underlyings for Naked Options
Another approach is to focus on low notional value underlying stocks when selling naked options.

Naked options carry clear advantages: we can collect more premium, which ultimately increases our probability of profit, and we have the ability to roll positions for a credit if the trade moves against us. Rolling an undefined risk naked option for a credit is far simpler compared to rolling a defined risk spread. With defined risk trades, to roll out for a credit, the stock price needs to land right near your short strike close to expiration. For naked undefined risk trades, the stock price can be anywhere, and as long as the option is in-the-money, I can roll it forward in time for a credit, with no long defined loss leg complicating the adjustment.

If we want to use naked options in a smaller account, target underlyings with very low share prices. Examples include Groupon, RIG for oil exposure, and CHK. These stocks trade at just five or six dollars per share. I can sell premium on these names, and even if the underlying goes bankrupt and drops to zero, if I sell a naked put at the $5 strike and collect 20 or 30 cents in premium, my maximum loss per single contract would only be roughly $470. This gives us a low-risk way to practice naked option mechanics using cheaper underlying instruments.

These two approaches have helped me the most when managing my small account portfolio and keeping individual position sizes controlled.

## Key 2: Limit Your Strategy Selection & Master a Few Core Plays
Let's move on to the next key, which is intentional strategy selection. There are countless different options strategies available because of all the variables we can manipulate, but narrowing your focus to a small set of strategies is critical.

You can draw a parallel to other areas of life: if you spread yourself too thin across work, hobbies, or learning objectives, you can never fully master any single one. The more you split your attention, the harder it is to deeply understand each individual skill. If we focus in on just a few key strategies, we can really hone in on entry criteria, exit triggers, and more importantly, how to manage losing positions when trades move against us.

For my own trading, my primary strategies are:
1. Selling naked puts on low-priced underlyings
2. Vertical spreads
3. Diagonal spreads, also known as poor man's covered calls and poor man's covered puts

I find diagonal structures extremely attractive for small accounts because they allow me to take a directional bias on the underlying. If we're operating in a low implied volatility environment like we are right now, these trades also let us capture profits from any future implied volatility expansion.

When building your small account playbook, pick just one, two, or three strategies to master — whether they are directional or non-directional neutral plays. Master those core structures thoroughly through consistent practice until you fully understand every component of the trade lifecycle. Once you have consistent results with your core set, you can gradually branch out into more complex strategies like broken wing butterflies or ratio spreads.

You do not need an overly complicated strategy lineup to be successful. Many of the top traders featured on the Tasty Trade Network only stick to iron condors or strangles full-time. Their success comes from perfect execution and position management of those simple core strategies, not juggling dozens of advanced structures. When starting out with a small account, keep your strategy toolkit limited and focus on mastery above all else.

## Key 3: Cut Losses Early — Do Not Hold Losing Trades Indefinitely
The final critical rule for small account trading is cutting losses quickly. I learned this lesson from a major losing position early on in my $10,000 account journey.

I sold a naked put on GoPro when the stock was trading around $33; I chose the $30 strike put. I personally loved GoPro hardware and used it snowboarding, so I had a biased bullish outlook and assumed the stock could not drop meaningfully lower. Today, GoPro trades around $13 or $14. I failed to cut my loss as the price declined, and instead held the position, rolling it forward two or three times. I collected roughly $500 in extrinsic value through those rolls, which offset part of my loss, but when I finally closed the trade entirely, my total loss landed around $1,400 to $1,500.

Research from our team shows the optimal loss exit rule is to close losing positions once your net loss reaches two times the total credit you initially collected. If I had followed this rule on the GoPro trade, I would have cut my losses far earlier and saved approximately $1,200 to $1,300, leaving my account significantly more profitable than it stands today.

Controlling losses is absolutely imperative for smaller accounts. Larger accounts have the buffer to hold naked options through deep drawdowns, rolling repeatedly and waiting for a price recovery. On a $100,000 account, a $1,500 loss is negligible capital. On a $10,000 small account, that same loss wipes out nearly 10% of your total portfolio — a massive hit with very little room for error.

Small accounts do not have the luxury of waiting for losing trades to reverse. Capital tied up in a losing position cannot be deployed elsewhere for higher-probability opportunities. Instead of tying up buying power in a struggling trade, you can close the loss, free up that capital, and open a new position with a far better statistical chance of profit for roughly the same amount of premium income.

When we talk about cutting losses at two times credit received, we are referencing the net premium collected when opening the trade. As an example: suppose I sell a put and collect $2.00 in credit. My exit stop loss would trigger when the cost to buy back that put reaches $6.00 debit. Selling for $2 credit and closing for $6 debit creates a net loss of $4, which is exactly two times the original credit received.

When placing trades and setting stop-loss resting orders, calculate your gross exit price at three times your initial credit collected to hit that two-times net loss threshold. This hard rule removes emotional decision-making when positions move against you.
